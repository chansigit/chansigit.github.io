**D<sub>2</sub><sup>R</sup>** is a single number that says how repetitive a DNA
sequence is. It counts how many short words (k-mers) match themselves inside
one sequence, subtracts how many such matches a random sequence would have, and
normalizes the result to lie between 0 and 1. Because it only counts word
occurrences and ignores where they are, one formula catches tandem repeats,
interspaced repeats and noisy repeats alike.

```
D2R = sum over words w of [ X_w (X_w - 1) - E[X_w (X_w - 1)] ] / [ n (n - 1) ]
```

Here X<sub>w</sub> is the count of word *w* and *n* is the number of k-mer
positions. The expectation is taken under a background model with base
frequencies estimated from the data.

**Algorithms.** A single sequence is scored in one pass with a hash of k-mer
counts. For a whole genome, a sliding window updates the score in constant time
per step, because moving the window by one base removes one k-mer and adds one.
The 2.2 Mbp genome of *Archaeoglobus fulgidus* is scanned in about half a
second.

**Results.**

- On simulated sequences with planted repeats, D<sub>2</sub><sup>R</sup>
  separates repetitive from random sequences with an AUC of about 0.99 at k = 7,
  and stays robust for k from 5 to 10.
- Scanning bacterial and archaeal genomes shows CRISPR arrays as sharp peaks;
  all three known CRISPR regions of *A. fulgidus* are found.
- On 20 human gut metagenomic samples, using D<sub>2</sub><sup>R</sup> to
  filter reads before the CRISPR finder Crass cut total run time by roughly
  50 to 83 percent while keeping about 90 percent of the CRISPRs found by Crass
  alone.

I derived the statistic, wrote the algorithms, and released them as a Java read
filter, a C++ genome scanner and a Python module. The work was advised by
Michael S. Waterman and Fengzhu Sun, whose D<sub>2</sub> statistics for
comparing two sequences it extends to a sequence against itself.
