## One number for how repetitive a sequence is

D<sub>2</sub><sup>R</sup> counts how many short words (k-mers) match themselves
inside one sequence, subtracts how many such matches a random sequence would
have, and normalizes the result to lie between 0 and 1.

It only counts word occurrences and ignores where they are, so one formula
catches tandem repeats, interspaced repeats and noisy repeats alike.

## The statistic

```
D2R = sum over words w of [ X_w (X_w - 1) - E[X_w (X_w - 1)] ] / [ n (n - 1) ]
```

- X<sub>w</sub> is the count of word *w* in the sequence.
- *n* is the number of k-mer positions.
- The expectation is taken under a background model with base frequencies
  estimated from the data.

## Linear-time algorithms

- A single sequence is scored in one pass with a hash of k-mer counts.
- For a whole genome, a sliding window updates the score in constant time per
  step: moving the window by one base removes one k-mer and adds one.
- The 2.2 Mbp genome of *Archaeoglobus fulgidus* is scanned in about half a
  second.

## Results

- Simulated sequences with planted repeats: AUC of about 0.99 at k = 7, robust
  for k from 5 to 10.
- Bacterial and archaeal genomes: CRISPR arrays show up as sharp peaks. All
  three known CRISPR regions of *A. fulgidus* are found.
- 20 human gut metagenomic samples: filtering reads before the CRISPR finder
  Crass cut total run time by roughly 50 to 83 percent and kept about 90
  percent of the CRISPRs found by Crass alone.

## My part and the code

I derived the statistic, wrote the algorithms, and released them as a Java read
filter, a C++ genome scanner and a Python module:
[code on GitHub](https://github.com/XuegongLab/D2R_codes).
